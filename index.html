From a wiggle in the air to the algorithms in your earbuds

Sound starts as moving air. Your phone grabs that motion, turns it into numbers, cleans the numbers up, polishes them, sets the volume, and turns them back into moving air — thousands of times every second. Every step on this page is something you can see and hear. Press play and poke things.

1

Sound is just air, wiggling

When anything vibrates — a guitar string, your vocal cords, a speaker cone — it pushes the air next to it, then lets it spring back. Those pushes travel outward as a wave. Your eardrum feels the pushes, and your brain calls it sound.

Every sound wave, from a whisper to a rocket launch, is described by two main knobs:

  • Frequency — how many times the wave wiggles per second, measured in hertz (Hz). Fast wiggle = high pitch. Slow wiggle = low pitch.
  • Amplitude — how big the pushes are. Big pushes = loud. Small pushes = quiet.

🎛️ The Pitch & Loudness Lab

Press play, then drag the sliders. Watch the wave: squeeze it left–right and it wiggles faster (higher pitch). Stretch it up–down and it gets taller (louder).

The wave shape matters too — try the shape buttons. A flute is close to a pure sine wave; a buzzy square wave is the sound of old video games.

Human hearing range: roughly 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz. Below 20 Hz you feel the sound in your chest more than you hear it (that's the movie-theater rumble). Dogs hear up to about 45,000 Hz — which is why a dog whistle is silent to you but obvious to them.

2

Volume and the decibel trick

Your ears are weird: they don't measure loudness in straight steps. The quietest sound you can hear and a jet engine differ in power by a factor of about a trillion — yet your ear handles both. It does this by responding to ratios, not differences. Double the power doesn't sound twice as loud; it sounds like a small step up.

Engineers copy the ear with the decibel (dB): a ruler where every +10 dB means 10× the sound power, but only sounds about twice as loud. That's why volume controls, audio software, and noise laws are all written in dB.

How loud is loud?

Long exposure above 85 dB can damage hearing — which is why phones warn you when your earbuds stay cranked for hours.

🔊 The Volume Lab

This slider works the way real audio gear does: it doesn't set "amount of sound," it sets gain in decibels. Drag it down and watch the wave shrink — and notice how the numbers behave. Going from −10 dB to −20 dB halves how loud it sounds, but the wave's height only drops to about a third.

The three rules of dB worth memorizing: +3 dB ≈ double the power (a small step). +10 dB ≈ ten times the power, sounds about twice as loud. 0 dB on a volume control means "full blast, unchanged" — negative numbers mean "turned down from max," which is why volume sliders go down to −∞ (silence).

3

Mono vs. stereo: two ears, one soundstage

You have two ears, and your brain is constantly comparing them. If a sound arrives at your left ear a little louder (or a hair earlier) than your right, your brain concludes: that came from the left. Recording engineers exploit this ruthlessly.

Mono (short for monophonic) sends one identical channel to both ears — every sound seems to come from a single point in the middle of your head. Stereo sends two different channels, left and right, so sounds can be spread across an imaginary stage between your ears: singer in the middle, guitar leaning left, keyboards right, drums wide. That spread is called the soundstage.

The steering wheel is panning: for each sound, the mixer decides how much of it goes to the left channel vs. the right. Pan a guitar 100% left and, on headphones, it plays in your left ear only.

🎧 The Stereo Lab

Press play and drag the pan slider — the dot is where your brain places the sound, and the bars show how much each ear actually gets. Then hit the Mono button: the pan slider stops mattering, both bars go identical, and the soundstage collapses to a single point. That collapse is exactly what happens when stereo music plays through one small speaker.

Mono isn't a failure — it's a choice. Phone calls, podcasts, and voice assistants are mono on purpose: one voice, no stage needed, half the data. AM radio and old records were mono because one channel was all they had. The trap is the reverse: play a stereo mix through a single speaker and any sound panned hard to one side can nearly vanish.

4

Turning sound into numbers

A computer can't store a smooth wave — it can only store numbers. So a microphone's wave is measured at regular instants, and each measurement becomes one number. That measurement process is called sampling, and it has two settings:

  • Sample rate — how many measurements per second. CD audio: 44,100 per second. Video and most car/phone systems: 48,000.
  • Bit depth — how precise each measurement is. 16-bit audio can pick from 65,536 possible heights. 8-bit only gets 256 — that's the crunchy sound of retro games.

🔬 The Sampling Lab

The pale curve is the real wave. The dots are the samples — the only thing the computer actually keeps. The colored line is what the computer rebuilds from those dots. Now drag the sample rate down and watch the rebuilt wave start to lie.

⚠️ Aliasing! Too few samples, and the rebuilt wave invents a different, lower pitch that was never there. It's the audio version of wagon wheels appearing to spin backwards in movies.

The Nyquist rule (the one law of sampling): to capture a frequency honestly, you must sample at least twice as fast as that frequency. Humans top out near 20,000 Hz — that's exactly why CDs sample at 44,100 Hz, a little over double. Old phone calls sampled at only 8,000 Hz, which is why voices on them sound thin and muffled: everything above 4,000 Hz was thrown away.

🗜️ Lossy vs. lossless: the art of throwing sound away

Raw CD audio eats about 10 MB per minute — fine on a disc, painful to stream to a phone. Two families of formats shrink it:

  • Lossless (WAV, FLAC, ALAC) — every single sample survives. FLAC uses zip-style tricks to cut the file roughly in half, and decoding gives back the exact original numbers. Nothing is lost, ever.
  • Lossy (MP3, AAC, Ogg) — much smaller, because the encoder permanently deletes detail first. The trick is psychoacoustic masking: a loud sound hides quieter sounds at nearby pitches for a short moment (a drum hit covers a faint hiss right after it). If you wouldn't have heard it anyway, the encoder doesn't bother storing it.

The bitrate (kilobits per second) is the budget: lower bitrate = smaller file = more deleted. The first thing to go is always the highest frequencies. Play the chord and step the quality down — watch the sparkle bars on the right disappear.

Deleted is deleted: converting an MP3 back to WAV gives you a big file that still sounds like an MP3. That's why audio engineers archive everything lossless and export lossy copies only at the very end.

5

Seeing sound: the frequency view

Here's the plot twist of audio: almost no real sound is a single wave. A piano note is dozens of frequencies stacked together — a strong fundamental plus quieter harmonics on top. The recipe of "how much of each frequency" is what makes a piano sound different from a violin playing the same note.

A math tool called the FFT (Fast Fourier Transform) un-stacks any sound into its frequencies in real time. This matters because nearly every audio algorithm thinks in frequencies, not in waves — noise lives at certain frequencies, voices at others, and the FFT lets software treat them separately.

📊 Frequency Bars — live FFT

Pick a sound. The bars show how much energy sits at each frequency, low pitches on the left, high pitches on the right. A single pure tone makes one spike; noise splatters everywhere.

Low tone → one spike near 130 Hz
Chord → spikes at 262 + 330 + 392 Hz
Noise → energy at every frequency
Square-ish sounds add spikes at odd multiples — those extra spikes are harmonics

Pre-processing: clean it before you use it

PRE-PROCESSING happens right after sound is captured, before the main job (a voice call, speech recognition, a recording). Its goal: fix problems — remove junk that would confuse whatever comes next. Garbage in, garbage out.

🧹 Hear the clean-up: hiss removal + noise gate

Below is a short tune buried in hiss, the way a cheap microphone hears it. Two classic pre-processing tools attack it:

  • Low-pass filter — hiss lives at high frequencies; the tune doesn't. Chop the highs, keep the tune.
  • Noise gate — anything quieter than a threshold gets muted completely, so pauses between notes go silent instead of hissing.
The graph shows the waveform you'll hear. Flat spots are where the gate slammed shut on the hiss between notes.

Echo cancellation: subtracting a sound you already know

On a video call, your speaker plays the other person's voice — and your microphone hears it and sends it right back, so they hear themselves echo. The fix is clever: the software knows exactly what the speaker just played, so it subtracts a copy of that sound from what the microphone picked up. What's left is (mostly) just your voice.

More pre-processing regulars

  • Automatic gain control (AGC) — rides the volume of a microphone so a quiet talker and a loud talker come out even.
  • Noise reduction — estimates the steady background (fan hum, road noise) from the FFT view and turns just those frequencies down.
  • Sample-rate conversion — re-measures a wave at a different rate, e.g. 44,100 → 48,000 Hz, so two systems can talk to each other.
  • Beamforming — several microphones compare arrival times to "aim" at one talker and ignore the rest.

Why it matters

Speech recognition, voice assistants, and call quality all live or die here. A voice assistant that hears you + TV + echo guesses words badly. Clean the input first, and every algorithm after it gets an easier job — that's the whole philosophy of pre-processing.

7

Post-processing: polish it before it plays

POST-PROCESSING happens at the end of the chain, on sound that's about to reach your ears. Pre-processing asks "is it clean?"; post-processing asks "does it sound good — and is it safe and even?" These are the tools music producers, podcast editors, and car audio engineers use all day.

🎚️ Equalizer (EQ): volume knobs for frequencies

An EQ is just the FFT idea in reverse: instead of looking at frequencies, you turn whole bands of them up or down. Crank the bass on a song and you're boosting everything below ~200 Hz. Below, static noise plays through a 3-band EQ — drag the sliders and watch the response curve bend. (Use the balance slider too: it shifts sound between your left and right ears.)

🗜️ Compressor: the automatic volume babysitter

Movies whisper dialogue, then explode. A compressor watches the level and automatically turns down anything that crosses a threshold — loud parts get squashed toward the quiet parts, so the whole thing feels more even (and a final safety stage, a limiter, hard-stops anything that would clip into distortion). Drag the threshold down and watch the wave's peaks get flattened.

Ratio is fixed at 4:1: for every 4 dB the input goes over the threshold, the output only rises 1 dB. Podcasts and radio use heavy compression so voices stay upfront; classical music uses almost none, on purpose.

✂️ Clipping: when "too loud" breaks the wave

Digital audio has a hard ceiling, called 0 dBFS ("decibels full scale") — the biggest value a sample can hold. Push a wave louder than the ceiling and its peaks have nowhere to go: they get sliced flat. That flat-topping is clipping, and your ears hear it as harsh, buzzy distortion — because a clipped sine wave is partway to becoming a square wave, extra harmonics and all (remember Section 5).

Drag the drive up. The pale wave is what the sound wants to be; the teal wave is what survives the ceiling. This is exactly what the compressor and limiter above are trying to prevent — though guitarists clip on purpose, into amplifiers designed to make it sound musical instead of broken.

🏛️ Reverb & effects: fake spaces

Reverb is thousands of tiny echoes bouncing off walls — it's how your brain knows "big hall" vs "tiny bathroom." Software adds it artificially by smearing a copy of the sound over time. Play the beeps, then switch the hall on.

More post-processing regulars

  • Mixing — balancing many sounds at once (music + navigation voice + phone call) with a volume for each.
  • Ducking — automatically dipping the music for a few seconds while a notification or directions speak.
  • Loudness normalization — making every song/video play at the same perceived loudness so you're not lunging for the volume knob.
  • Fade / balance — shifting sound front/back and left/right between speakers.

Pre vs post, in one line

Pre-processing fixes sound on its way in, so machines can understand it. Post-processing shapes sound on its way out, so humans enjoy it. Same toolbox (filters, gains, FFT) — different job, different end of the pipeline.

8

Speakers: different sizes for different pitches

Everything so far happened as numbers and electricity. A speaker is where it becomes air again — a cone (or dome) that pushes back and forth. Here's the problem: one size can't do everything well. A big, heavy cone is great at slow, powerful pushes (bass) but far too sluggish to wiggle 15,000 times a second. A tiny, light dome is the opposite. So most speakers are a team, and a circuit called a crossover plays traffic cop — sending each frequency to the driver built for it.

Think of it like a relay team: the woofer carries the heavy, slow laps, the tweeter sprints the fast ones, and the midrange — where your ears are most sensitive — handles the all-important middle.

BIG CONE · 6–12 in · LOW

🔉 Woofer

Job: bass and low notes. Big cone, long throws — it moves a lot of air slowly.

You know it from: kick drums, bass guitar, movie rumble you feel in your chest.

40 Hz~500 Hz
MEDIUM CONE · 3–6 in · MIDDLE

🔊 Midrange

Job: the middle — and the most important job of all, because voices live here.

You know it from: singing, dialogue, guitar, piano — almost everything you actually follow.

300 Hz~5 kHz
TINY DOME · ~1 in · HIGH

✨ Tweeter

Job: high pitches. Tiny and feather-light, so it can wiggle tens of thousands of times a second.

You know it from: cymbals sizzling, birdsong, the sparkle and detail on top of music.

3 kHz20 kHz
ONE DRIVER · TRIES EVERYTHING

🎯 Full-range

Job: one driver attempts the whole range alone. Simple, cheap, and coherent — but a compromise at both ends.

You know it from: phone & laptop speakers, cheap Bluetooth speakers, TV speakers. Thin bass, soft top end.

A subwoofer is the woofer's extreme cousin: an extra-big driver (often in its own box) for only the lowest ~20–100 Hz — the part you feel more than hear.

How many speakers? Reading the numbers: 2.0, 2.1, 5.1…

The numbers aren't marketing — they're a count. First number = regular speakers around you. Number after the dot = subwoofers. So 2.1 = 2 speakers + 1 subwoofer. A third number (as in 5.1.2) = speakers above you, for height. The diagrams below are top-down room maps — the screen is at the top, you sit at the bottom.

2.1 — Stereo + sub

2 speakers · 1 sub

What you get: proper left–right stereo (a guitar can sit left, a singer center-ish) plus a sub handling all the deep bass the small speakers can't.

Found in: desk setups, soundbars with a wireless sub, most car systems (roughly), good TV setups on a budget.

5.1 — Surround sound

5 speakers · 1 sub

What changes: a dedicated center speaker locks dialogue to the screen, and two surround speakers behind you put rain, crowds, and footsteps around you — sound now has a front and a back.

Found in: home-theater systems, cinemas (bigger versions of the same idea), gaming setups.

Dolby Atmos — sound in 3D

5.1.2 and up · + height

What changes: Dolby is the company; Atmos is its 3D format. Instead of “play this in the left channel,” sounds are stored as objects with a position in space — a helicopter isn't in a speaker, it's above and moving. Your system works out which speakers (including ceiling or up-firing ones) to use.

Written as: 5.1.2, 7.1.4 — that last number is how many height speakers. Cinemas, high-end soundbars, and newer cars and earbuds (spatial audio) use it.

Don't mix the two ideas up: driver types (tweeter / midrange / woofer) split sound by pitch inside one speaker box. Channel counts (2.1 / 5.1 / Atmos) split sound by position around the room. A single 5.1 front speaker might itself contain a tweeter and a woofer inside it — both splits happening at once. And in a car? One zone, a handful of speakers, and the same rules: small tweeters up by the windshield for sparkle, bigger woofers in the doors for bass.

Microphones: speakers running in reverse

Every speaker above turns electricity into air movement. A microphone does the exact opposite — air movement in, electricity out — and the two classic designs are mirror images of the speaker parts you already know:

🎤 Dynamic microphone

A light diaphragm — the speaker cone, shrunk — has a coil of wire glued to it, floating inside a magnet. Sound pushes the diaphragm, the coil wiggles in the magnetic field, and that motion generates a tiny voltage: the signal. No power needed, and nearly indestructible.

Found in: live stages, podcast mics, karaoke — anywhere it might get dropped or shouted into.

🎙️ Condenser microphone

Here the diaphragm is one plate of a tiny capacitor, held a hair's width from a fixed back plate. Sound wiggles the gap, the capacitance wiggles with it, and electronics turn that into the signal. A feather-light diaphragm hears faint, fast detail a dynamic mic misses — but it needs power (a battery, or "phantom power" from the mixer) and hates rough handling.

Found in: recording studios, voice-over booths, measurement mics — anywhere detail matters more than toughness.

Your phone uses a third design: a MEMS microphone — the same capacitor idea etched into a silicon chip smaller than a grain of rice, which is how one phone fits several of them (bottom, top, back) for the beamforming you met in pre-processing.

Try tracing it: you say "call Mom" in a noisy car. Every section of this page is one box in that chain.

Latency: the delay hiding inside every sound

Latency is the time between a sound entering a system and the result reaching your ears. Computers don't process samples one at a time — they collect them into a chunk called a buffer, process the chunk, and pass it on. A bigger buffer means more delay, but also more safety: make the buffer too small and the processor occasionally can't keep up, so the audio crackles or drops out. Every stage in the pipeline adds its own little delay, and they stack up.

How much is too much depends on the job. A musician monitoring their own playing notices anything over about 10 ms. In games and video, delays past roughly 50–100 ms start to feel disconnected — mouths move before words arrive. On a phone call, you can tolerate a few hundred milliseconds before the conversation turns into people talking over each other.

⏱️ The Buffer Lab: trading delay for safety

At the 48,000 Hz sample rate from Section 4, one sample lasts 1/48,000 of a second — so a buffer's delay is just its size divided by 48,000. Drag the buffer size and watch the total one-way latency grow (buffer + converters + a typical driver allowance). Small buffers feel instant but risk crackle; big buffers are safe but sluggish.

ALSA: the words under the hood

Every Linux and Android device — phones, laptops, car head units — plays sound through ALSA, the Advanced Linux Sound Architecture. When an app "plays audio," it really hands ALSA chunks of numbers and lets ALSA feed the hardware. Engineers who work on that layer use a small vocabulary of precise words. Learn these six and you can read almost any audio bug report.

It helps to think of them as zoom levels — each one is built out of the one before:

Sample one number

The single measurement from Section 4: the wave's height for one channel at one instant. A sample by itself carries no time and no other channels — it's just a number, like +0.42.

Frame one instant, all channels

One sample from every channel at the same moment — a stereo frame is an L+R pair that must stay glued together. That's why "sample rate" is really a frame rate: 48 kHz means 48,000 frames per second, however many channels ride along.

Channel one position

One stream aimed at one speaker position: left, right, center… A frame holds exactly one sample per channel, so a 5.1 frame (6 channels) carries 6 samples, and mislabeling channels is how "front-left" ends up playing in the rear.

Period the chunk

Apps don't feed ALSA one frame at a time — they hand over a period (say 512 frames) at once. Every time the hardware finishes playing a period, it taps the app on the shoulder: "one period done — refill, please." Small periods = frequent wake-ups = low delay but more CPU work.

Buffer the ring

Several periods arranged in a circle. The app writes at one point of the ring, the hardware reads at another, and both chase each other around the loop. Buffer size = periods × period size — it's the cushion of ready-to-play sound, and a big chunk of the latency from Section 10 lives right here.

Threshold the starting line

The start threshold: how many frames must be sitting in the buffer before playback begins at all. You've met this word twice — the noise gate and the compressor both fire at a threshold. High threshold: playback starts later, but with a bigger cushion. Too low, and the first tiny hiccup runs the buffer dry.

🔄 The Ring Buffer Lab: don't let the music starve

The app writes in bursts — one whole period whenever the system gets around to it. The sound card drains the ring at a perfectly steady rate. If the reader ever laps the writer, the buffer hits empty mid-playback: that's an underrun (ALSA calls it an XRUN), and you hear it as a click, a gap, or a robot glitch. Press start, watch the ring fill past the threshold tick before the sound card will even begin — then stall the app and see whether your cushion survives.

Real systems live inside this trade-off: a car head unit might run 512-frame periods with a few periods of buffer for responsive beeps, while a music player uses a fat buffer so a bumpy road (or a busy CPU) never starves it. Latency vs. safety — the same bargain as Section 10, now with ALSA's names on it.

Why 16 kHz for voice calls, 24 kHz for voice recognition — and 48 kHz for music?

If 48 kHz captures everything, why would anyone sample slower? Because of what the sound actually contains. Look at where voices live on the frequency map:

📏 Who needs which frequencies?

Each bar is the slice of the frequency spectrum that job actually uses. The ▲ markers are Nyquist limits (rate ÷ 2, from Section 4): a sample rate only keeps what's below its line — so each job picks the smallest rate whose line clears its bar.

  • Telephone voice was squeezed into 300–3,400 Hz to fit old copper lines — an 8 kHz rate clears it, barely. That's the muffled "phone voice," and why "fifty" and "sixty" get confused: the crisp consonant sounds that separate them live higher up.
  • Voice calls today (HD voice) run at 16 kHz, capturing up to ~7–8 kHz. Nearly everything that makes a voice sound like that person — pitch, warmth, most consonants — sits below 8 kHz. A 48 kHz rate would record three times as many samples describing frequencies that carry no voice at all.
  • Voice recognition (VR) and assistants use 16 or 24 kHz. At 24 kHz (superwideband, up to ~12 kHz) the engine gets the finest consonant detail — the hissy edges of s, f, sh — and tells them apart more reliably. Still half the samples of 48 kHz.
  • Music & media genuinely reach 20 kHz — cymbals, harmonics, air — so they keep the full 48 kHz. Nothing is wasted here; the content is really there.

👂 Hear what each rate keeps

A voice-like sound (low buzzing harmonics plus the hissy "sizzle" of consonants up high) plays continuously. Each button applies the Nyquist limit of that sample rate. Watch the right-hand bars of the frequency view — that's the detail each rate throws away, and the reason a phone call sounds like a phone call.

Why not just record everything at 48 kHz and be safe? Every sample costs: the echo canceller, noise reduction, codec, and speech models must chew through each one, and a call must also send them over the network. 48 kHz is 3× the samples of 16 kHz — roughly 3× the compute, battery, and bandwidth — spent describing silence above the voice. Real systems mix rates on purpose: the voice path runs at 16/24 kHz, the media path at 48 kHz, and a sample-rate converter (Section 6) translates where they meet.
