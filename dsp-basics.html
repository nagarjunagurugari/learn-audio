DSP Basics, Visualized

Digital Signal Processing sounds like graduate-level math. It isn't. Nearly everything a DSP chip does — every EQ, echo, and noise canceller in your car, earbuds, and phone — boils down to one humble operation, repeated millions of times per second:

output = (sample × weight) + (sample × weight) + (sample × weight) + …

This page builds that idea up step by step: filters, convolution, the frequency domain, fixed-point math, and the brutal real-time deadline every audio algorithm races against. Every section has a lab — drag the sliders and watch the math move.

01

Filters are just weighted averages

Imagine a temperature sensor that jitters: 21.2°, 19.8°, 22.5°, 20.1°… The true temperature isn't leaping around — the measurement is noisy. The oldest trick in the book: average the last few readings. The jitter cancels, the trend survives. That trick, applied to audio samples, is a filter.

Audio is just a stream of numbers — samples. A moving-average filter replaces each sample with the average of itself and its recent neighbors. Noise wiggles up and down randomly, so averaging shrinks it. The smooth musical signal changes slowly, so it passes through almost untouched. Fast wiggles removed, slow shapes kept — that's exactly what a low-pass filter means.

The reveal: nothing forces the weights to be equal. Give recent samples more say and old samples less, and you shape which frequencies get removed. A filter whose output is a weighted sum of recent inputs is called an FIR filter — Finite Impulse Response. “Finite” because it only ever looks back at a fixed window of the past.
y[n] = w₀·x[n] + w₁·x[n−1] + w₂·x[n−2] + … + wN−1·x[n−N+1]

Each weight w is called a tap or coefficient, and the whole list of weights is the filter's kernel. Designing a filter = choosing the weights. A moving average is just the special case where every weight equals 1/N. Keep this equation in mind — by Section 3 you'll see it's secretly the engine of all DSP.

02

FIR vs. IIR: memory vs. feedback

An FIR filter has amnesia by design: shout one loud click into it (an impulse) and the effect washes out after exactly N samples, when the click slides out of the window. But there's another way to build a filter — let the output feed back into itself:

y[n] = b₀·x[n] + a₁·y[n−1] + a₂·y[n−2] + …

That's an IIR filter — Infinite Impulse Response. One click echoes into the output, a fraction of it feeds back, echoes again, and so on forever (fading each round). Like a microphone held near its own speaker, feedback gives enormous power from tiny hardware — and the same danger.

FIR — feedforward only

  • Always stable — no feedback, so it can never run away.
  • Predictable phase — easy to keep timing perfectly even.
  • Hungry — a sharp filter may need hundreds of taps, each one a multiply-add per sample.

IIR — with feedback

  • Super efficient — a handful of coefficients can do what takes an FIR hundreds of taps.
  • Can go unstable — if the feedback gain reaches 1, the echo never fades. Past 1, it explodes.
  • Touchy — tiny rounding errors recirculate forever (remember this in Section 5).

Real products use both. The EQ in a music app is usually built from IIR “biquad” sections — two feedback coefficients doing the work of a long FIR. Crossovers and linear-phase mastering tools lean on FIR. The engineer's question is always the same trade: efficiency (IIR) vs. guaranteed stability (FIR).

03

Convolution: the one operation to rule them all

Look again at the FIR equation: multiply recent samples by weights, add them up, slide forward one sample, repeat. That slide-and-multiply-add pattern has a name — convolution — and it is quite possibly the most important operation in all of signal processing.

Here's the recipe, one output sample at a time:

1 · FlipReverse the kernel left-to-right. (Yes, really — convolution flips. In symmetric filters like ours you can't tell, but the math insists.)
2 · SlidePark the flipped kernel over the input, starting at the left edge, and slide it one sample at a time.
3 · Multiply-addAt each stop, multiply every overlapping pair and add the products. That sum is one output sample.

The payoff: convolution doesn't care what the kernel means. Change the kernel and the same flip-slide-multiply-add machine becomes a completely different effect. One operation, endless personalities:

🎚 Filtering

Kernel = a few taps (like [¼, ½, ¼]). Convolving smooths, sharpens, or picks out a frequency band. Every filter from Section 1 is a convolution.

🏛 Reverb

Kernel = a recording of a real room's echo pattern (its impulse response) — thousands of taps of decaying reflections. Convolve dry audio with it and the audio sounds like it was performed in that room.

🔊 Crossovers

Convolve the same song with a low-pass kernel → feed the woofer. Convolve with a high-pass kernel → feed the tweeter. Two convolutions split one signal into the bands each speaker handles best.

And it's not just audio: image blurring, edge detection, and the “convolutional” in convolutional neural networks are this same operation wearing different clothes. Learn flip-and-slide once, and you own a piece of half of modern computing.

04

Why the frequency domain exists

Convolution has one problem: for long kernels it's slow. A one-second reverb kernel at 48 kHz has 48,000 taps — that's 48,000 multiply-adds for every single output sample, 2.3 billion per second of audio. There has to be a cheaper way, and there is — by changing your point of view.

Any signal can be re-described as a stack of sine waves: which frequencies are present, and how strong each one is. That's the signal's frequency domain picture (Part 1's FFT section showed how to compute it). And in that picture, something magical happens:

Convolution in time = multiplication in frequency. Filter a signal by transforming it, multiplying each frequency bin by the filter's response at that frequency, and transforming back. A 48,000-tap nightmare becomes a row of simple one-by-one multiplications.

That's why an EQ is so natural in the frequency domain: “turn the bass up” literally means “multiply the low bins by 1.5.” Noise reduction works the same way — estimate which bins are mostly speech and which are mostly hiss, multiply the hissy ones toward zero.

05

Fixed-point vs. floating-point: why DSPs think in integers

Your laptop's CPU loves floating-point numbers — values like 3.14159 × 10⁻⁴ that can be astronomically large or microscopically tiny. Dedicated DSP chips (like the HiFi-class cores in car head units and earbuds) often prefer fixed-point: plain integers, where you simply agree that the decimal point sits in a fixed place. The integer 16384 in “Q15” format means 0.5, because the scale is 2¹⁵ = 32768 and 16384 / 32768 = 0.5.

Why bother? Integer hardware is smaller, cheaper, and sips power — decisive when the chip lives in an earbud or must run for pennies inside a car. But fixed-point exacts two prices:

Price 1 — Quantization noise

Only whole steps exist. A smooth value like 0.50003 must snap to the nearest step, and that snap error is a tiny bit of noise added to your audio. Fewer bits → bigger steps → more noise. Rule of thumb: each bit buys ≈ 6 dB of quiet.

Price 2 — No headroom

Q15 tops out just below 1.0. Multiply two loud samples, or let an IIR's feedback build up, and the result can exceed the ceiling — that's overflow. Fixed-point code wraps around (giant positive becomes giant negative: a violent click) or saturates if you pay for the guard. Floating-point, with its enormous range, almost never overflows.

06

The real-time budget: racing the sample clock

Offline, slow is fine — a photo filter can take all afternoon. Audio is different. At 48 kHz, a new sample arrives every 20.8 microseconds, whether your math is done or not. Miss the deadline and the output buffer has nothing new to play: you get the gap, click, or robotic stutter Part 1 called an underrun.

Nobody processes one sample at a time, though — that's too much bookkeeping overhead. Instead, audio moves in frames (blocks): collect, say, 480 samples = 10 ms of sound, then process the whole block in one burst. The deal is simple and absolute:

The frame deadline: all processing for a frame must finish within the frame's duration. 480 samples at 48 kHz buys you 10 ms of compute — on a 600 MHz DSP that's 6 million clock cycles, your entire budget. Every filter tap, every FFT, every multiply spends from it.

Engineers budget this in MIPS (millions of instructions per second) or as a percentage: “the echo canceller takes 180 MIPS of our 600.” And the frame size itself is a trade — bigger frames are more efficient (less overhead, FFT-friendly) but add latency, because you wait to fill the frame before you start. This is the cold-vs-continuous latency story from Part 1, now with a price tag.

Lab

Build a processing chain, beat the clock

You have one 10 ms frame on a 600 MHz DSP (6.0M cycles). Toggle the algorithms your product team wants and watch the budget. Then run the race: if total compute exceeds the frame, frames start dropping — those are underruns, and listeners hear every one.

When the math doesn't fit: there's no partial credit. The chip can't “try harder” — options are to optimize the code, shrink the algorithm (fewer taps, shorter FFT), lower the sample rate, or buy a faster DSP. This is why Sections 4 and 5 matter in real products: FFT-domain processing and tight fixed-point code aren't academic elegance, they're how the whole feature list fits inside 10 milliseconds.

And with that, the loop closes. Weighted averages became filters; filters became convolution; convolution forced us into the frequency domain; the frequency domain and integer math keep the whole thing affordable; and the sample clock collects payment 48,000 times a second. That's DSP.
